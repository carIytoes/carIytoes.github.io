RUN — UHNF·RIE

RIE Process Optimization of Silicon (SF₆/Ar)

Summer research, 8 weeks · UH Nanofabrication Facility
Plasma etch Bayesian optimization Random forest surrogate Central Composite Design AFM metrology

Background

Reactive-ion etching (RIE) is one of the most process-sensitive steps in semiconductor fabrication — small shifts in gas chemistry, RF power, or pressure can change etch rate, anisotropy, and surface roughness in ways that are expensive to characterize by brute-force experimentation. This project worked on an SF₆/Ar etch recipe for silicon on an Oxford Instruments Plasmalab System 100 ICP tool at the University of Houston Nanofabrication Facility, with the goal of building a more efficient way to search that process space than traditional one-factor-at-a-time tuning.

Approach

Rather than running an exhaustive parameter sweep, I designed a physics-informed Bayesian optimization framework: a Central Composite Design (CCD) laid out the initial experimental points across the parameter space (RF power, pressure, gas flow ratio), and a random forest surrogate model was trained on the resulting measurements to predict etch outcomes at unsampled points. The optimizer then used that surrogate to suggest which parameter combination to test next — the same core idea used in hyperparameter tuning for machine learning models, applied instead to a physical fabrication process.

"Physics-informed" meant the search space and the surrogate model weren't treated as a black box — known relationships between RF power, ion bombardment energy, and etch anisotropy shaped how the parameter bounds and priors were set, rather than letting the optimizer explore blindly.

Measurement & Metrology

Etch outcomes were characterized using atomic force microscopy (AFM) to measure surface roughness, alongside etch depth measurements, feeding results back into the optimization loop after each run.

Results & What I Learned

The honest outcome: direct experimental results were inconclusive. Photolithography and surface-preparation steps upstream of the etch introduced enough variability that isolating the effect of the RIE parameters themselves was difficult within the project's timeframe. So instead of a clean "we improved etch rate by X%" result, the real contribution ended up being the optimization framework itself — built specifically to be robust to noisy, imperfect experimental data, which is closer to the reality of process engineering than a textbook clean dataset ever is.

That distinction matters to me: a framework that degrades gracefully under messy real-world conditions is arguably more useful than one that only works when everything upstream is perfect.
